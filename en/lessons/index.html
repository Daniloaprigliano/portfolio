01

Method before marks

The problem is rarely effort: it is method. Students study for hours and get little back because they have no procedure to follow.

I am a tenured teacher of Italian and Latin at a secondary school in Milan, with a PhD in Romance philology; I have been teaching for fifteen years. Every week I set and mark tests, Latin translations and essays, so I know exactly where marks are lost. Lessons start from there.

For Latin I use a visual method. Every sentence is taken apart in colours: first the verb, then whoever performs the action, then the subordinate clauses, one at a time. Translation stops being guesswork and becomes a procedure that can be checked step by step. The dictionary comes last, to choose the right word, not to look for a way out.

Caesar, cum id nuntiatum esset, maturat ab urbe proficisci.

  • 1 · verb
  • 2 · subject
  • 3 · subordinate clause
  • 4 · complements

“When this was reported to him, Caesar hastened to set out from Rome” (adapted from Caesar, De bello Gallico, I, 7).

The same principle applies to Italian. For text analysis I teach a stable procedure: paraphrase, structure, figures, context, interpretation. It works for a poem by Montale as well as for a page of Manzoni, and it holds up in the first written paper of the maturità. For essays we work on the outline and the argument before drafting, because that is where an essay succeeds or fails.

03

How a lesson works

  1. We start from real work. A marked test or a recent essay, not generic exercises: it is the quickest way to see where marks are lost.
  2. Every session ends with a task. A translation, a text, an exercise, which I mark in writing before the next lesson.
  3. Before tests, mock tests. With real timings and on the kind of paper the teacher has announced. For students who need to recover a failing grade, I set a calendar up to the end-of-term assessment.
